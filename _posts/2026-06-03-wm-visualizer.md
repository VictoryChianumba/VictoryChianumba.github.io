title: "SAE feature ranking by activation magnitude fails on world models — a case study"
date: 2026-06-03
---

The top-ranked feature by activation magnitude in my trained SAE is the most strongly anti-correlated feature with the on-screen event a user is most likely to look for.

That's the finding. The rest of the post is what it means and how I came to be sure of it. The substrate is IRIS, a transformer world model trained on Atari Breakout, and the SAE is a standard TopK sparse autoencoder trained on its layer-5 residual stream. Both are healthy by the usual diagnostics. The mismatch isn't in the model or the SAE — it's in what activation-magnitude ranking surfaces on a substrate where state evolves slowly.

The setup. I trained the SAE on layer 5 residual-stream activations from IRIS playing Breakout: 2048 features, 8× expansion factor, L1 coefficient 2.0, harvested across roughly 4,700 frames at 17 token positions each (about 80k activation vectors total). The trained SAE has an L0 of around 14–15, which sits in the band usually called interpretable, and its top features show the sharp magnitude falloff a healthy SAE should — 3.5, 2.7, 2.1, 1.2, 0.5 for the top five — rather than the flat 2.2–2.7 distribution of an undertrained one. By the standard diagnostics, the SAE is fine. None of what follows is a story about a bad SAE.

I built an interactive tool around it that lets a user pause the rollout, browse SAE features by activation magnitude (the LLM-SAE default), pin features onto a canvas, and intervene on them by pushing the feature's direction into the residual stream during the model's imagined-next-frame generation.

*The feature explorer canvas, with #1364 pinned and steered while the rollout panel compares baseline and intervened imagined frames.*

The tool worked, the interventions had measurable effects, and the immediate complaint when I started using it was: the features feel decoupled from what's happening on screen. I'd watch the top-firing feature during a paddle-ball collision — exactly the moment I expected a "collision detector" to fire — and it would sit flat. Or I'd see strong feature activation while the ball was clearly in mid-air, nowhere near anything to interact with. Either the SAE features were broken, mislabeled, or I was misreading the timing. To pin down which, I ran a diagnostic battery.

The first thing to rule out was that I was looking at the wrong thing. Test 1 checked whether the game frame and the activations shown beside it were even from the same model step. They almost weren't — the displayed frame was being captured after the environment stepped while the activations described the pre-step observation, so the frame led its activations by exactly one step. Reproducible across two episodes, with a clean +1 → 0 lag fix once I caught it. Worth dwelling on because the bug was inside a single WebSocket message: I'd bundled the frame and activations together to guarantee they render in lockstep, and assumed that meant they described the same step. They didn't. A frame-and-activations bundle that travels together is not the same as a frame-and-activations bundle that describes the same step, and a one-frame offset is precisely the artifact a human reads as "the feature fires just before the event, not during it."

With mechanical causes ruled out — Test 2 confirmed the activation extraction itself was bit-exact deterministic on identical inputs — the question became what the features actually represent. Test 3 measured it directly. For each of the top 50 features by overall activity, I computed mean activation during paddle-ball collision frames versus mean activation during ball-in-air frames, then the difference. The frames were labeled from the full-resolution Atari source rather than from the model's 64×64 input, since (a separate finding) the ball is sub-pixel at the model's input resolution and pixel-based labeling on the model's own observation isn't reliable. Two independent episodes scored the same way.

The ranking turned out to be strongly structured and highly reproducible. The difference scores correlated at Spearman +0.977 across the two episodes, with 7-of-10 overlap in the top features. Of the top 50 by activity, about 15 were clearly collision-correlated (Δ > 0.1), 22 were flat (absolute Δ ≤ 0.1), and 13 were anti-correlated (Δ < −0.1). Genuine collision detectors exist and are well-separated: feature #1199 fires only at collision (mean activation 1.40 at contact, 0.00 in flight); feature #120 is similar (1.23 vs 0.05). These are clean event detectors.

And then there's #1364, the single most-active feature in the SAE in this trained checkpoint, the one the discovery panel shows first when the tool opens. Its mean activation at collision is 0.82. Its mean activation in mid-air is 5.57. It is the most strongly anti-collision feature in the entire top 50. It tracks the ball through its flight and goes quiet at the moment of contact. A user doing exactly what I did — watching the top-firing feature during a collision — sees it stay flat, because its job is not to detect contact. Its job is to track the ball through the air.

A clarification before going further, because the way I've been writing this implies more stability than it has. Feature IDs in an SAE are arbitrary column indices assigned during training, so "#1364" is a label specific to this trained checkpoint rather than a stable identifier of the air-tracking concept. When I retrain the SAE on the same data with different seeds, the same kinds of features reliably reappear — a paddle-movement feature, one or two collision detectors, a ball-tracker — but they land in different columns each time. The specific feature that's highest-magnitude in one run isn't necessarily the highest in another. There's also good reason from prior work on LLM SAEs to expect these features are polysemantic, so a "collision detector" probably fires for several related things rather than for collisions cleanly; I haven't measured this directly on the features here but it's the kind of complication you'd expect on this scale of SAE.

So the "#1364 is the air-tracker" framing is a snapshot of one checkpoint, not a stable identity. What's more robust is the pattern: across retrainings, an air-tracker-like feature reliably exists, it reliably accumulates more total activity than the collision detectors, and the magnitude ranking reliably surfaces it first. The "decoupling" I'd been complaining about wasn't a bug in the SAE or the tool — it was the ranking surfacing the wrong kind of feature, and surfacing a different specific feature each time I retrained, while the user was trying to build up stable knowledge of "which feature does what."

The mechanism is mundane once you see it. Magnitude rankings score by something close to duration × strength of firing. A feature that fires continuously through the several-second arc of a ball flight accumulates far more total activity than a feature that fires only during the handful of frames of paddle contact. Persistent state-trackers beat brief event detectors in any magnitude-based race, even when the event detectors are real and clean. On a language model this isn't a problem because each token is a discrete decision point and "top firing now" is a reasonable proxy for "important to this decision." On a world model where frames are highly correlated and the underlying state evolves slowly, "top firing now" surfaces whichever feature has been firing through the current slow state, not whichever feature is responding to a momentary event.

There's a sharper version of this once you account for feature-identity instability. Magnitude ranking doesn't just point at the wrong kind of feature; it points at different wrong features across retrainings, while the underlying concepts the user actually wants to find shift around in feature-ID space. The user is trying to build up stable knowledge of "feature #X does Y" across sessions, the magnitude ranking is reshuffling which feature it shows first each session, and even retraining changes which feature ID the persistent concepts land in. The LLM-SAE convention of citing features by number works on LLMs because the convention assumes both that the right feature is being surfaced and that the user can return to that feature across sessions. Neither assumption is safe here.

There's a sharper version of this finding I want to draw out, because it ended up being usable. While building alternative rankings I'd implemented temporal stability — ranking features by how consistently they fire over a recent window rather than how hard. Of the five most temporally-stable features in the SAE, four (#27, #1221, #1773, #1789) appeared in the collision-correlated top 10 from Test 3, versus roughly chance for magnitude. The substrate-adapted ranking surfaced the event-tied features that magnitude buried. I want to be careful here — this is one comparison on one feature set, and the stability-vs-collision result is suggestive rather than conclusive — but the direction is consistent. The Δ ranking it builds on (Spearman +0.977 across episodes) is the reproducible part; "stability beats magnitude for surfacing event detectors" is one comparison on top of that.

I tried to go one step further and rank features by causal importance — intervene on each feature, measure how much the model's imagined rollout diverges, rank by effect size. This is the most defensible definition of importance because it measures the thing you care about directly. The first version produced an encouraging result: on a sample of 24 active features, causal importance correlated with activation magnitude at Spearman +0.56–+0.64 across two independent runs, and the single most causal feature was #1364. Cross-run top-5 overlap was 4/5. The reading at the time was "magnitude is a moderate proxy for causal importance; it predicts the top but not the ordering below it." Plausible, clean, the kind of finding that ships.

A verification pass unwound it. The pipeline was injecting interventions as scale × activation × direction — magnitude-relative, so that a feature already firing strongly received a proportionally larger perturbation. This is the correct semantics for the live intervention tool, where a user steers a feature relative to its current activation. It's exactly wrong for an importance ranking, because a bigger perturbation produces a bigger downstream change, so the score becomes a re-expression of activation. #1364, with activation 5.9, was being injected at roughly magnitude 30 while typical features got about 5. Its causal "rank 1" was an artifact of being hit six times harder than its competitors. The encouraging correlation with magnitude wasn't a finding about causality tracking magnitude; it was the pipeline measuring magnitude twice. The trap is that this is invisible at the per-feature level — every feature's score looks like a clean intervention measurement — and only shows up when you correlate the score against activation across the whole population. That cross-check (corr(score, activation) = +0.37) is what caught it; the per-feature math gave no reason to run it.

The fix was one line — fix the injection at unit norm regardless of feature activation. The confound went away (corr(score, activation) dropped to roughly zero). But removing the confound exposed a deeper problem. The Atari environment reset and the trained near-argmax policy are effectively deterministic, so every run from the same seed warmed up to the same game state. The causal score wasn't noisy because of CUDA; it was reflecting essentially one state, perfectly. Re-seeding couldn't diversify a deterministic pipeline. Diversity had to come from sampling different points along a playthrough — time-sampling states rather than re-running from the same reset.

With genuine state diversity, the signal was real but not robust enough to rank ~2000 features. Cross-set Spearman went from approximately zero on a single state, to +0.33 with 5 states, to +0.49 with 18 states — diminishing returns, and reaching a threshold I'd trust as a ranking (Spearman ≥ 0.6) would have required many tens of states. The pipeline turned out to be CPU-dispatch-bound rather than GPU-bound — each autoregressive forward pass is small enough that GPU time is dominated by Python overhead — so more compute didn't fix it either. I'd pre-committed to a Spearman ≥ 0.6 threshold before seeing the numbers, +0.49 fell below it, and causal importance didn't ship as a discovery ranking. It survives in the tool as a characterization tool — the top handful of causally-potent features reproduce reliably, including the air-tracker — but not as a primary navigational surface.

That part of the project ended with a negative result, and I want to be precise about what the negative actually shows. It's not "causal importance doesn't work for SAE features in world models." It's "causal importance on a deterministic-environment world model is single-state-fragile, and the diversification needed to make the ranking reproducible runs into a dispatch-bound serial bottleneck that doesn't yield to additional GPU." Both of those are usable observations for the next person trying to build something similar. The transferable methodological lesson is narrower and worth stating plainly: any causal-importance pipeline on SAE features needs to use magnitude-independent injection, and the failure mode is invisible unless you correlate the resulting score against activation across the whole feature population. Without that cross-check, the pipeline silently re-implements magnitude ranking and your "causal importance" numbers tell you nothing new.

The picture across the project, summarized: I built a working SAE feature explorer for IRIS-class world models. The most concrete finding is that activation magnitude — the LLM-SAE default — surfaces persistent state-trackers over brief event detectors on this substrate, with an air-tracker-like feature as the worked example. The specific feature ID doing the tracking shifts across retrainings, but the pattern holds — magnitude reliably surfaces something persistent before it surfaces anything brief. Temporal stability surfaces the event detectors that magnitude buries; the comparison is one-shot but the underlying Δ ranking is reproducible across episodes. Causal importance is real but single-state-fragile on a deterministic environment, with a magnitude-coupling failure mode that's only visible at the population level. Several other observations dropped out of building the tool — tokenizer compression limits pixel-space behavioral readout on imagined frames, single-frame priming produces near-static rollout baselines that limit what divergence-from-baseline can claim, argmax decoding sets the floor on intervention smoothness regardless of SAE quality — but I'd categorize those as engineering notes for the next person building similar tooling rather than as research findings.

The limits are worth being explicit about. This is one game (Breakout), one model (IRIS), one SAE configuration, one tokenizer. The 2048-feature SAE is small relative to the 32K+ that's typical for LLM work, and the 80k vectors behind it is on the low side. None of this is at the scale where a finding generalizes by itself. The magnitude-ranking observation is the one I'd most expect to replicate elsewhere, because it follows from a structural difference between LLMs and world models (token-level decision points versus slow-evolving state) rather than from anything specific to IRIS or Breakout. The other findings are observations from one corner; they'd want re-running on other substrates before any of them count as transferable.

The practical takeaway: don't reach for activation magnitude as the default importance signal on a substrate with slowly-evolving state, and don't treat feature IDs as stable references across retrainings — they're checkpoint-specific in a way that interacts badly with the LLM-SAE convention of citing features by number, especially when the underlying concepts persist across retrainings but the IDs carrying them don't. If you're building tooling around an LLM-SAE convention, be ready to test whether the convention actually transfers.
